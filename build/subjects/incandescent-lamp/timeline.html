As of 2026-10-02. Every event is a shared node (build/nodes/lamp-*.yaml) that holds the date, its basis and precision, the place where a source names one, and the sources; this file only says where each dot sits. Times are set to noon UTC and are drawn at day, month or year precision; the node's `date_basis` and, where the date is uncertain, its `window` say how the date was reached. A patent's grant date is used unless the label says filed. Events marked disputed have sources that disagree, as named in the detail. Claimed events with no dated record (Goebel 1854, Swan 1850s, de la Rue 1840) are not drawn as events; the earliest dated traces of the Goebel claim (1882) are. Places are given only where a source names them; the court rulings are placed at state level because the opinions do not name the sitting city.

11812 (year only)1812: Davy's account of the carbon arc and the 2000-plate battery is in print
Davy, Elements of Chemical Philosophy, pp. 85-86: with the Royal Institution's battery of 2000 double plates, pieces of charcoal about an inch long and one sixth of an inch across, brought within a thirtieth or fortieth of an inch, gave 'a bright spark', and 'more than half the volume of the charcoal became ignited to whiteness'; drawn apart, 'a constant discharge' formed 'a most brilliant ascending arch of light' of at least four inches. The book gives no date for the experiment. An arc, not an incandescent lamp. The demonstration date (1808-1810) is not in the book.		single documentn:lamp-1812-davy-publishes-carbon-arc:0
221 Aug 1841 (date only)21 Aug 1841: de Moleyns, British patent No. 9053
Abridgment of British patent 9053 (A.D. 1841, August 21), Frederick de Moleyns, 'the production or developement of electricity, and the application of electricity for the attainment of illumination and motion'. Its second head is 'a means of generating the electric light, by constantly supplying the electrodes at their junction, with pulverized charcoal' from a tube; the electrodes are two coils of platinum wire, the lower enclosing spongy platinum, and 'the whole arrangement is enclosed in an exhausted glass globe'. The abridgment does not use the words 'glow' or 'incandescent'. The Supreme Court (1895) calls it a patent for an incandescent electric lamp using finely pulverized boxwood charcoal or plumbago. The full specification was not read. 61873 (year only)1872-73: Lodygin's carbon-rod lamps in St Petersburg
Fontaine (1878): 'in 1873 a Russian physicist, M. Lodyguine, resuscitated both [lighting by incandescence and its principle], and invented a new lamp'. People and bodies involved

  • Humphry Davy: Royal Institution chemist; his 1812 book describes the carbon arc.
  • Frederick de Moleyns: British patent No. 9053, 1841: the earliest electric-light patent with a lamp in an exhausted globe found.
  • John Wellington Starr / Edward Augustin King: British patent No. 10,919, 1845, issued to King as a communication; a later court and later sources name Starr as the inventor.
  • de Changy: Worked in Belgium per Fontaine (Howell and Schroeder 1927 call him a French civil engineer with a Belgian patent); sealed platinum lamps reported by Jobard in 1858.
  • Alexander Lodygin: Russian; carbon-rod lamps reported 1872-73; Academy prize 1874; later metal-filament patents.
  • Henry Woodward and Mathew Evans: Toronto; Canadian patent 3,738 (1874); Woodward's US patent 181,613 (1876).
  • Joseph Swan: Newcastle; carbon-rod lamp described Dec 1878; a lamp shown at the Lit and Phil about two years before Oct 1880 (his own account); a lamp with a very thin carbon shown in his Oct 1880 lecture.
  • Thomas Edison and his laboratory: Menlo Park; Charles Batchelor kept the notebook of the 22 Oct 1879 test.
  • William Sawyer and Albon Man: New York; won the Patent Office interference on paper carbons (early 1885); their patent was held invalid in 1889 and the Supreme Court affirmed in 1895.
  • Lewis Latimer: Carbon-making patent 252,386 (1882) and, with J. V. Nichols, a lamp-mounting patent (1881), the first assigned to the United States Electric Lighting Company.
  • Heinrich (Henry) Goebel: New York mechanic; said he made lamps in the 1850s; the earliest dated traces of that claim in the court record are from 1882.
  • Judges Wallace, Lacombe, Colt, Hallett, Seaman, Bradley, Brown: Wrote the opinions read for this dig (Brown for the Supreme Court).
